Timeline of the PlayStation Network Cyberattack

Pre-Event Background: Legal Action and Retaliation

January 2010: George Hotz ("Geohot"), a well-known hacker, releases a method to jailbreak the PlayStation 3 (PS3), allowing users to bypass security restrictions, install custom firmware, and potentially use pirated software on the console.

February 2011: Sony files a lawsuit against Hotz, accusing him of violating the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) and infringing on Sony’s intellectual property rights. This move angers the hacking community, who view the lawsuit as corporate overreach. Sony’s legal actions attract the attention of Anonymous, a hacktivist group focused on fighting censorship and defending digital freedom.

Early April 2011: Anonymous initiates Operation Sony to retaliate against the lawsuit. Anonymous conducts Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attacks on various Sony websites and services, temporarily disrupting operations. This was intended as a protest, though Anonymous claims it did not seek to steal or damage user data during these attacks.

Main Event: The PSN Cyberattack

April 17–19, 2011: Hackers infiltrate Sony’s PlayStation Network (PSN) servers, using vulnerabilities in Sony’s security infrastructure to gain unauthorized access. During this period, attackers access sensitive data, including usernames, passwords, and possibly encrypted credit card information for millions of accounts.

April 20, 2011: Sony shuts down the PlayStation Network without initially explaining the reason to the public, citing only “technical difficulties.” This outage affects all 77 million PSN users worldwide, cutting off access to online gaming, media streaming, and account services.

Discovery and Announcement of the Breach

April 22, 2011: Sony confirms to the public that there has been an “illegal and unauthorized intrusion” into the network but does not yet disclose the nature or extent of compromised data.

April 26, 2011: Sony formally announces that personal data from millions of accounts has been compromised. They reveal that names, addresses, birthdates, PSN IDs, passwords, security answers, and possibly credit card information were accessed. Sony advises users to monitor their financial accounts and change their passwords immediately.

April 27, 2011: Following this announcement, Sony experiences significant backlash from the public and media, as well as scrutiny from government agencies worldwide. Users express concern over the delayed notification and potential financial risks.

Response and Recovery

April 28, 2011: Sony initiates a formal investigation, partnering with cybersecurity experts and law enforcement agencies, including the FBI. Sony’s reputation suffers, and there is widespread media coverage questioning Sony’s cybersecurity practices.

April 30, 2011: In a letter to Congress, Sony states that it is working with the FBI to track down the attackers and outlines their plans to enhance security. The letter reveals that the attack may have included additional Sony Online Entertainment (SOE) services, raising concerns that more accounts could be affected.

May 1, 2011: Sony officially discloses that the total number of affected accounts is 77 million, making this one of the largest data breaches at the time. They confirm that the breach exposed unencrypted personal data and potentially encrypted credit card data, although they cannot confirm if credit card data was compromised.

Customer Communication and Service Restoration

May 2, 2011: Sony CEO Kazuo Hirai publicly apologizes to PSN users and announces that the company will implement new security measures to restore the network safely.

May 5, 2011: Sony announces plans for a "Welcome Back" program to compensate users, including offering free games, PlayStation Plus subscriptions, and free identity theft protection.

May 14, 2011: Sony begins to restore PSN services in phases, first in North America, then in Europe and other regions. The phased rollout includes updated security protocols, and Sony implements additional security features like more robust firewalls and monitoring tools.

May 24, 2011: Sony completes the restoration of PSN services worldwide, having overhauled its security infrastructure and customer communication channels.

Long-Term Repercussions and Legal Consequences

June 2, 2011: Sony appears before the U.S. Congress to address the breach, facing questions about their delayed response and lack of robust data protection. Sony admits that its security measures were insufficient and outlines new cybersecurity improvements.

June 2011: Sony is fined £250,000 by the U.K.'s Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) for failing to protect customer data effectively. This fine underscores global scrutiny of Sony’s handling of the breach and pushes the company to reinforce its data protection policies.

July 2011: Sony faces multiple class-action lawsuits in the U.S. and other regions from customers who claim negligence in protecting their data and delaying breach notifications. Sony agrees to settle, which includes financial compensation to users and commitment to further improve security practices.

Post-Breach Reforms and Broader Industry Impact

September 2011: Sony creates a new Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) position, hiring cybersecurity experts to oversee data protection and IT infrastructure security.

2011–2012: Sony introduces stronger encryption standards, increases network monitoring, and implements multi-factor authentication for user accounts. They also enhance customer communication protocols, aiming to rebuild trust and demonstrate a commitment to security.

2012 and Beyond: The PSN breach prompts the gaming and technology industries to re-evaluate their own security measures. Other companies in the gaming industry adopt stricter data protection practices, spurred by the realization that customer data security is a critical component of corporate reputation and customer trust.
